> "Interest rates act on financial valuations the way gravity acts on matter: the higher
> the rate, the greater the downward pull."
>
> — Warren Buffett

The quote gets repeated constantly. The mechanism behind it gets explained far less often,
which is a shame, because understanding *how* rates transmit into valuations tells you
something much more useful than the directional claim.

I want to be clear about what this post is not: it is not a forecast. I have no idea where
rates go next, and neither does anyone else with a reliable record. What follows is
structural — how the machinery works, regardless of which way it turns.

## The Mechanism

Every asset is worth the present value of the cash it will produce. Converting future cash
into present value requires a discount rate, and that discount rate is built on top of the
risk-free rate — the yield on government bonds.

When the risk-free rate rises from 2% to 5%, every discount rate in every valuation model
on earth rises with it. The cash flows have not changed. The businesses have not changed.
The arithmetic converting those cash flows into a price has changed, and that is sufficient
to reprice everything.

This is why rate moves feel indiscriminate. They are. The repricing is mechanical before
it is analytical.

## Why Long-Duration Assets Move Most

Here is the part that matters more than the direction.

Consider two businesses. **Business A** generates $10 of free cash flow per share every
year and grows barely at all — a mature utility, say. **Business B** generates $1 today but
is expected to grow at 25% annually for fifteen years before maturing.

Nearly all of Business A's value sits in cash flows arriving in the next decade. Most of
Business B's value sits in cash flows arriving in years ten through twenty-five.

Discounting compounds. A cash flow arriving in year twenty gets discounted twenty times
over. Raise the discount rate by three points and that distant cash flow loses a far
greater share of its present value than a cash flow arriving next year.

The practical result: when rates rise, high-growth businesses whose value is concentrated
in the distant future fall much harder than mature businesses generating cash today. Not
because their prospects deteriorated, but because the arithmetic of discounting is
non-linear in time.

This is **duration**, and it is the single most useful concept for understanding why some
stocks halve during a rate cycle while others barely move.

## The Second Effect: Competition for Capital

The discount rate mechanism is the direct effect. There is an indirect one that is
arguably larger.

When government bonds yield near zero, an investor wanting any return at all is pushed
into equities, property, credit, or something more exotic. The famous acronym was TINA —
there is no alternative. Capital flooded into risk assets partly on merit and partly
because the alternative paid nothing.

When the same bonds yield 5%, there is very much an alternative. A guaranteed 5% from a
sovereign issuer sets a genuine hurdle. An equity now has to justify itself against a
risk-free return, and marginal ideas that cleared the bar at zero no longer clear it.

This effect works through flows and sentiment rather than through any model, which makes
it messier and less predictable — but it is often what drives the violence of the initial
repricing.

## What This Means Practically

**Do not forecast rates.** I have never seen a durable edge here, including from people
whose full-time job it is. Building a thesis that requires a rate call means the analysis
is only as good as the weakest input, and that input is a guess.

**Do use a consistent discount rate.** The temptation when rates fall is to lower the
discount rate and watch valuations expand. This is technically correct and practically
dangerous, because it means your estimate of intrinsic value swings with the bond market.
I use a discount rate around 9–10% for most businesses regardless of the rate environment.
When rates are low this makes me look too conservative. That is the point — the
conservatism is doing work precisely when everyone else is relaxing.

**Do understand your portfolio's duration.** If most of your holdings are valued on cash
flows a decade out, you own a rate-sensitive portfolio whether or not you think of it that
way. That is an acceptable position. Being unaware of it is not.

**Do treat rate-driven selling as an opportunity, carefully.** When a business falls 40%
because discount rates rose and nothing about the business changed, that is exactly the
dislocation value investors wait for. The caution is that rising rates sometimes also
signal real deterioration ahead — higher borrowing costs, weaker demand, refinancing risk.
Separating the mechanical repricing from the fundamental one is the actual work.

## The Deeper Point

Rates change the *price* of future cash flows. They do not change *which businesses will
produce them*.

A company with a durable moat, high returns on capital and a long reinvestment runway is
the same company at 2% rates and at 6% rates. What changes is what you have to pay for it,
and therefore what return you will earn.

This is why I find rate anxiety mostly misdirected. The question is never "what will rates
do?" It is "at today's price, am I being adequately compensated for owning this business?"
That question is answerable. The other one is not.
