Every deep dive I publish follows the same structure. Rather than describe that structure
abstractly, this post works through it completely.

**An important note before we start:** the company below, *Meridian Fasteners*, does not
exist. Every figure is invented. I have done this deliberately. If I used a real company,
the natural instinct would be to skip to the conclusion and ask whether the stock is a
buy. Using a fictional business keeps attention on the reasoning, which is the only part
that transfers to your own analysis.

---

## 1. What Does This Business Actually Do?

Meridian Fasteners distributes industrial fasteners — bolts, screws, rivets, anchors — to
manufacturers and maintenance operations across North America. It stocks roughly 400,000
distinct items across 340 branches and, increasingly, places small automated inventory
units directly inside customer facilities.

The fasteners themselves are commodities, manufactured by third parties and available
from dozens of suppliers. Meridian manufactures nothing. On the surface this looks like a
terrible business: no proprietary product, no patents, no brand that any end consumer has
ever heard of.

That surface reading is wrong, and understanding why is the whole analysis.

## 2. How Does It Make Money?

A fastener costs a few cents. The assembly line it stops when it runs out costs thousands
of dollars an hour.

This asymmetry is the entire economic engine. Meridian's customers are not buying bolts;
they are buying the certainty that the bolt will be there. That certainty is worth far
more than the component, which is why Meridian earns a gross margin around 46% on
products that are functionally interchangeable with a competitor's.

The onsite inventory units deepen this. Once Meridian installs automated dispensing units
inside a customer's plant, integrates with their procurement system, and trains their
staff on it, switching to a competitor means re-tooling a working process to save a
trivial amount on a line item that barely registers in the customer's cost base.

Nobody does that. This is a **switching cost moat**, and it is one of the most durable
kinds because the cost of switching is measured in operational risk rather than money.

## 3. Is the Moat Real, and Is It Widening?

The test of a moat is not whether you can construct a story for it. It is whether the
numbers behave the way the story predicts.

If switching costs are real, we should observe: high and stable ROIC, gross margins that
hold or expand through recessions, and customer retention that does not deteriorate when
competitors discount.

| Metric | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
|--------|------|------|------|------|------|
| Revenue growth | 4.1% | 11.8% | 6.2% | 3.4% | 5.9% |
| Gross margin | 45.9% | 45.2% | 46.1% | 46.4% | 46.2% |
| Operating margin | 19.8% | 20.4% | 21.1% | 20.6% | 21.3% |
| ROIC | 27% | 29% | 31% | 29% | 32% |
| Onsite units installed | 12,400 | 14,900 | 17,600 | 20,100 | 23,400 |

Three things stand out.

**ROIC near 30% sustained over five years.** Against a cost of capital around 8%, this is
a business creating roughly 22 points of spread on every dollar deployed. Competition
should have eroded this. It has not.

**Gross margin stability through a demand slowdown.** In 2024, revenue growth fell to
3.4% — clear evidence of a soft industrial cycle. Margins did not fall. A business without
pricing power discounts to defend volume when demand weakens. Meridian did not need to.

**Onsite installations compounding at ~17% annually.** This is the moat being actively
constructed. Each unit installed is a customer relationship that becomes progressively
harder to dislodge.

The moat is real and it is widening.

## 4. Where Could This Go Wrong?

The bull case is now clear, which is exactly when I need to work hardest on the bear case.

**Customer concentration in cyclical end markets.** Roughly 38% of revenue comes from
heavy manufacturing and automotive. A severe industrial recession hits volumes hard.
Margins may hold, but a 20% revenue decline still compresses operating profit sharply
given the fixed cost of 340 branches.

**Reshoring reversal.** Part of recent growth reflects manufacturing returning to North
America. If that reverses, Meridian's addressable market shrinks structurally rather than
cyclically. This is the risk I find hardest to size.

**Onsite saturation.** At some point every customer worth installing a unit for has one.
Growth then depends on winning share from competitors or expanding internationally —
neither of which carries the same economics. When does the runway end? I do not know, and
that uncertainty belongs in the valuation.

**Large-format distributor entry.** A competitor with genuine scale could subsidise onsite
installations to buy share. Meridian's defence is that switching costs protect the
installed base, not that the model cannot be copied.

## 5. Management and Capital Allocation

Over the last decade Meridian generated roughly $4.2bn in cumulative free cash flow and
deployed it as follows: 48% to buybacks, 31% to dividends, 21% to organic expansion. No
acquisitions.

The absence of acquisitions is a genuine positive. A distributor with a high-return
organic model and a management team that resists empire-building through M&A is
demonstrating the discipline that most capital allocation destroys.

The buybacks are more mixed. Share count fell about 14% over the decade, but repurchases
were heaviest in 2021 and 2022 when the multiple was highest, and lightest in 2024 when
the stock was cheapest. This is the common pattern and it is value-destructive at the
margin. Not disqualifying, but worth noting: this is a management team good at operations
and mediocre at market timing.

Insider ownership sits near 3% of shares outstanding, most of it purchased rather than
granted. Compensation is tied to ROIC and earnings per share rather than revenue. Both are
what I want to see.

## 6. What Is It Worth?

Assume the following, all deliberately conservative relative to the historical record:

- Revenue growth of 5% annually for ten years, below the five-year average
- Operating margin holding at 21%, with no assumed expansion
- Tax rate of 24%
- Maintenance capex at 2.5% of revenue
- Discount rate of 9%, above the estimated cost of capital
- Terminal growth of 2.5%, roughly long-run nominal GDP

These assumptions produce an intrinsic value of approximately **$96 per share**.

That single number is the least important output of the exercise. What matters is the
sensitivity:

| | 4% growth | 5% growth | 6% growth |
|---|---|---|---|
| **8% discount** | $98 | $112 | $128 |
| **9% discount** | $84 | $96 | $110 |
| **10% discount** | $73 | $83 | $95 |

The plausible range runs from roughly $73 to $128. That spread — a factor of 1.75x
between the pessimistic and optimistic corners — is the honest expression of what I know.
Anyone presenting a DCF as a single precise figure is either not showing you this table
or has not built it.

As a sanity check: at $96, the business trades near 22x free cash flow, a 4.5% FCF yield.
For a business earning 30% ROIC with a visible reinvestment runway, that is defensible
but not obviously cheap.

## 7. The Decision

Applying a 30% margin of safety to the $96 midpoint gives a buy price around **$67**.

That is below even the most pessimistic corner of the sensitivity table, which tells me
something useful: this is a high-quality business unlikely to reach a price that offers a
large statistical discount, absent a market-wide dislocation.

So the honest conclusion is not "buy" or "avoid." It is: **understand it now, and be ready.**
The analysis has value even when it does not produce a transaction, because the work is
done when the opportunity arrives. The investors who buy well during panics are almost
never the ones doing the analysis during them.

Meridian goes on the [watchlist](/blog/watchlist/) at $67.

## The Structure, Extracted

Stripped of the specifics, every deep dive answers seven questions in this order:

1. **What does it do?** — described plainly enough that a non-specialist would follow
2. **How does it make money?** — the actual economic mechanism, not the revenue line
3. **Is there a moat, and is it widening?** — the story, then the numbers that test it
4. **What could go wrong?** — argued as forcefully as the bull case
5. **Is management rational?** — judged by capital allocation history, not by rhetoric
6. **What is it worth?** — a range with visible sensitivities, never a point estimate
7. **What do I do?** — including the legitimate answer of "nothing, for now"

The order matters. Valuation comes sixth deliberately. Building the model first creates
an anchor, and every subsequent piece of evidence gets unconsciously bent to support it.
Understand the business, then find out what you would pay.
