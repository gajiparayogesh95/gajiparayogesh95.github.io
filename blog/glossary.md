Financial writing hides a lot behind vocabulary. This page defines every term I use
regularly, in plain language, with the formula where one exists.

<div class="glossary-group" id="b">
<div class="glossary-letter">B</div>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Book Value</dt>
<dd>The accounting value of a company's equity: total assets minus total liabilities. Useful for asset-heavy businesses like banks and insurers, largely meaningless for asset-light software or brand-driven businesses where the real assets never appear on the balance sheet.
<span class="formula">Book Value = Total Assets &minus; Total Liabilities</span></dd>
</dl>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Buyback (Share Repurchase)</dt>
<dd>A company buying its own shares, reducing share count and increasing each remaining shareholder's claim on earnings. Value-creating only when shares are bought below intrinsic value &mdash; which, in practice, is not when most companies buy them.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

<div class="glossary-group" id="c">
<div class="glossary-letter">C</div>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Capital Expenditure (Capex)</dt>
<dd>Cash spent on physical assets like property, equipment and technology. Worth separating into <em>maintenance</em> capex (required to keep the business running as-is) and <em>growth</em> capex (investment in new capacity). Companies rarely split these out, so estimation is part of the analysis.</dd>
</dl>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Circle of Competence</dt>
<dd>The set of businesses you genuinely understand well enough to judge. Munger's point is that the boundary matters far more than the size &mdash; knowing where your competence ends is what protects you.</dd>
</dl>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Cost of Capital</dt>
<dd>The blended return that a company's debt and equity providers require. It is the hurdle rate: a business earning returns below its cost of capital destroys value even while reporting accounting profits.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

<div class="glossary-group" id="d">
<div class="glossary-letter">D</div>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)</dt>
<dd>A valuation method that projects a business's future free cash flows and discounts them back to today's value. Theoretically correct and practically fragile &mdash; small changes to the growth rate or discount rate produce enormous changes in output. Best used to understand what assumptions the current price implies, rather than to produce a precise target.</dd>
</dl>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Dilution</dt>
<dd>The reduction in existing shareholders' ownership when a company issues new shares, typically through stock-based compensation or capital raises. A company growing earnings 10% while growing share count 8% is delivering far less than it appears.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

<div class="glossary-group" id="e">
<div class="glossary-letter">E</div>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Earnings Power Value (EPV)</dt>
<dd>Greenwald's method of valuing a business on its sustainable current earnings with no growth assumed. Because it removes growth from the equation, it is far more robust than a DCF &mdash; and any value above EPV must be justified by a moat.
<span class="formula">EPV = Normalised Operating Earnings &times; (1 &divide; Cost of Capital)</span></dd>
</dl>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>EBITDA</dt>
<dd>Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. Widely used and widely abused. Depreciation represents real assets wearing out; ignoring it flatters capital-intensive businesses. Munger's description &mdash; "bullshit earnings" &mdash; is uncharitable but not wrong.</dd>
</dl>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Enterprise Value (EV)</dt>
<dd>What it would cost to buy the entire business, including assuming its debt. More useful than market capitalisation when comparing companies with different capital structures.
<span class="formula">EV = Market Cap + Total Debt &minus; Cash</span></dd>
</dl>
</div>

<div class="glossary-group" id="f">
<div class="glossary-letter">F</div>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Free Cash Flow (FCF)</dt>
<dd>The cash a business generates after paying for the capital investment needed to maintain its operations. This is the money genuinely available to shareholders, and it is much harder to manipulate than reported earnings.
<span class="formula">FCF = Operating Cash Flow &minus; Capital Expenditure</span></dd>
</dl>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Free Cash Flow Yield</dt>
<dd>Free cash flow divided by market capitalisation &mdash; the most direct measure of what you are getting relative to what you are paying. Compare it to the risk-free rate to judge whether you are being compensated for equity risk.
<span class="formula">FCF Yield = Free Cash Flow &divide; Market Cap</span></dd>
</dl>
</div>

<div class="glossary-group" id="g">
<div class="glossary-letter">G</div>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Goodwill</dt>
<dd>The premium paid above the fair value of identifiable assets in an acquisition, recorded as an asset. Large and growing goodwill balances often indicate a company that has systematically overpaid. Impairment charges are the delayed admission.</dd>
</dl>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Gross Margin</dt>
<dd>Revenue minus cost of goods sold, as a percentage of revenue. A durable, high gross margin is one of the clearest quantitative signatures of pricing power.
<span class="formula">Gross Margin = (Revenue &minus; COGS) &divide; Revenue</span></dd>
</dl>
</div>

<div class="glossary-group" id="i">
<div class="glossary-letter">I</div>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Intrinsic Value</dt>
<dd>What a business is actually worth &mdash; the discounted value of all cash it will produce over its remaining life. Unknowable precisely; estimable within a range. The entire discipline consists of narrowing that range and then paying meaningfully less than the low end.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

<div class="glossary-group" id="m">
<div class="glossary-letter">M</div>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Margin of Safety</dt>
<dd>The discount between the price paid and the estimated intrinsic value. It exists to absorb analytical error, bad luck and unforeseeable change. The less certain the estimate, the wider the margin needs to be.</dd>
</dl>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Moat</dt>
<dd>A structural advantage that allows a business to sustain high returns on capital despite competition. The durable sources are switching costs, network effects, cost advantages from scale, intangible assets such as brands and patents, and counter-positioning. Anything else is usually temporary.</dd>
</dl>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Mr. Market</dt>
<dd>Graham's allegory: imagine a business partner who offers each day to buy your stake or sell you his, at wildly varying prices depending on his mood. You are free to ignore him. His usefulness is that occasionally he offers a genuinely foolish price.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

<div class="glossary-group" id="n">
<div class="glossary-letter">N</div>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Net Debt</dt>
<dd>Total debt minus cash and equivalents. A company with net cash has optionality in a downturn; one with heavy net debt has fragility.
<span class="formula">Net Debt = Total Debt &minus; Cash &amp; Equivalents</span></dd>
</dl>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Net-Net</dt>
<dd>A Graham-era term for a stock trading below its net current asset value &mdash; current assets minus <em>all</em> liabilities. Extremely rare in developed markets today, but still occasionally found in small caps and in Japan.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

<div class="glossary-group" id="o">
<div class="glossary-letter">O</div>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Owner Earnings</dt>
<dd>Buffett's preferred measure: reported earnings plus depreciation and amortisation, minus the capital expenditure genuinely required to maintain competitive position. Closer to economic reality than either net income or EBITDA.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

<div class="glossary-group" id="r">
<div class="glossary-letter">R</div>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Return on Invested Capital (ROIC)</dt>
<dd>How efficiently a business converts capital into profit &mdash; the single most informative metric in fundamental analysis. A business sustaining ROIC well above its cost of capital almost certainly has a moat; the analytical work is identifying what it is and whether it will last.
<span class="formula">ROIC = After-Tax Operating Profit &divide; (Equity + Net Debt)</span></dd>
</dl>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Reinvestment Runway</dt>
<dd>How much additional capital a business can deploy at high rates of return before saturating its market. High ROIC with a long runway is the mathematical engine behind multi-decade compounding; high ROIC with nowhere to reinvest produces a good dividend and little else.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

<div class="glossary-group" id="s">
<div class="glossary-letter">S</div>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Scuttlebutt</dt>
<dd>Philip Fisher's term for primary research conducted outside the filings: talking to customers, former employees, suppliers and competitors to learn what the numbers do not show.</dd>
</dl>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Second-Level Thinking</dt>
<dd>Howard Marks's distinction. First-level thinking: "this is a good company, so I should buy it." Second-level thinking: "this is a good company, but everyone knows that and the price already reflects it &mdash; so what do I see that they do not?"</dd>
</dl>
</div>

<div class="glossary-group" id="t">
<div class="glossary-letter">T</div>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Terminal Value</dt>
<dd>The estimated value of all cash flows beyond the explicit forecast period in a DCF. It frequently represents 60&ndash;80% of the total valuation, which is precisely why DCF outputs should be treated as ranges rather than answers.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

<div class="glossary-group" id="w">
<div class="glossary-letter">W</div>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Value Trap</dt>
<dd>A stock that appears statistically cheap but is cheap for a reason &mdash; usually structural decline in the underlying business. The defining error of naive value investing, and the reason business quality matters as much as price.</dd>
</dl>

<dl class="glossary-term">
<dt>Working Capital</dt>
<dd>Current assets minus current liabilities &mdash; the capital tied up in day-to-day operations. Businesses that collect cash before delivering their product operate on <em>negative</em> working capital, effectively being financed by their customers.</dd>
</dl>
</div>
