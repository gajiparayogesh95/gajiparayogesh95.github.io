Checklists are not a substitute for judgement. What they do is prevent the specific
failure mode that costs investors most: getting excited about one attractive feature of
a business and skipping the questions that would have revealed the problem.

Atul Gawande's observation about surgery applies directly here — the errors that harm
people are rarely errors of ignorance. They are errors of *ineptitude*, where the
knowledge existed but was not applied. This list exists to make me apply it.

I work through every block below before any purchase. If I cannot answer a question,
that is itself the answer.

## 1. Do I Understand This Business?

<div class="checklist-block">
<h3>Circle of competence</h3>
<ul>
  <li>Can I explain how this company makes money in two sentences, without jargon?</li>
  <li>Can I name the top three competitors and explain why a customer picks this company over them?</li>
  <li>Do I understand what the business will plausibly look like in ten years?</li>
  <li>Could I explain the bear case as persuasively as the bull case?</li>
  <li>Am I relying on anyone else's analysis for a conclusion I could not reach myself?</li>
</ul>
</div>

## 2. Is the Business Good?

<div class="checklist-block">
<h3>Economics and competitive position</h3>
<ul>
  <li>Has return on invested capital exceeded roughly 12&ndash;15% consistently over a full cycle?</li>
  <li>Can I name the specific source of the moat &mdash; switching costs, network effects, scale economies, intangibles, or counter-positioning?</li>
  <li>Is that moat widening, stable, or narrowing? What evidence supports my view?</li>
  <li>Have gross margins been stable or expanding over five or more years?</li>
  <li>Does the company have pricing power &mdash; has it raised prices without losing volume?</li>
  <li>Is there a long runway to reinvest capital at high rates of return?</li>
</ul>
</div>

## 3. Are the Financials Sound?

<div class="checklist-block">
<h3>Balance sheet and cash generation</h3>
<ul>
  <li>Does operating cash flow track net income over time, or has it persistently diverged?</li>
  <li>Is free cash flow positive and growing across the cycle, not just in good years?</li>
  <li>Is interest coverage (EBIT / interest expense) comfortably above 5x?</li>
  <li>Could the business survive two consecutive bad years without raising capital?</li>
  <li>Are working capital trends consistent with the growth story, or deteriorating?</li>
  <li>Have I read the footnotes for off-balance-sheet obligations, pensions, and related-party transactions?</li>
  <li>Is share count flat or falling &mdash; and is stock-based compensation reasonable as a share of revenue?</li>
</ul>
</div>

## 4. Is Management Trustworthy and Rational?

<div class="checklist-block">
<h3>Capital allocation and incentives</h3>
<ul>
  <li>What has management done with cash over the past decade &mdash; and did those decisions create value?</li>
  <li>Do executives own meaningful stock purchased with their own money, not just granted?</li>
  <li>Are compensation targets tied to per-share value creation rather than revenue or headline EBITDA?</li>
  <li>Has management been candid about mistakes in past shareholder letters?</li>
  <li>Do reported "adjusted" figures exclude costs that are plainly recurring?</li>
  <li>Have they bought back stock when it was cheap and issued when it was expensive &mdash; or the reverse?</li>
</ul>
</div>

## 5. Is the Price Right?

<div class="checklist-block">
<h3>Valuation and margin of safety</h3>
<ul>
  <li>Have I estimated intrinsic value using at least two independent methods?</li>
  <li>What growth rate is the current price implying &mdash; and is it plausible?</li>
  <li>Does my margin of safety match the uncertainty in my estimate? (More uncertainty, larger discount.)</li>
  <li>What is the free cash flow yield, and how does it compare to the risk-free rate?</li>
  <li>Have I stress-tested the thesis: what does this look like if revenue falls 20%?</li>
  <li>Would I be comfortable buying more if the price fell 30% tomorrow?</li>
</ul>
</div>

## 6. Red Flags — Any One of These Stops the Process

<div class="checklist-block redflag-block">
<h3>Automatic disqualifiers</h3>
<ul>
  <li>Auditor has issued a going-concern qualification or resigned recently</li>
  <li>Frequent restatements, or a CFO who has changed more than twice in five years</li>
  <li>Revenue recognised well before cash is collected, with receivables growing faster than sales</li>
  <li>Serial acquisitions that keep producing "one-time" restructuring charges</li>
  <li>Management compensation tied primarily to share price over short windows</li>
  <li>Debt maturities concentrated within eighteen months and no committed facility</li>
  <li>The investment thesis requires a macro forecast to be correct</li>
  <li>I find myself adjusting assumptions upward to make the valuation work</li>
</ul>
</div>

## 7. Before I Click Buy

<div class="checklist-block">
<h3>Final discipline</h3>
<ul>
  <li>Have I written the thesis down in one page, including what would prove me wrong?</li>
  <li>Have I specified, in advance, the conditions under which I would sell?</li>
  <li>Is the position size appropriate to my conviction and to the uncertainty involved?</li>
  <li>Am I buying because the analysis says so, or because I am afraid of missing out?</li>
  <li>Have I waited at least 48 hours between finishing the analysis and acting on it?</li>
</ul>
</div>

---

That last item is the one I most often want to skip, and the one that has saved me the
most money. The urge to act immediately after finishing an analysis is almost always
emotional rather than analytical. Good opportunities are rarely destroyed by two days
of patience.
